Project 01 · International scouting data

International Talent Pathways

Of every international amateur who started pro ball, how many made it, and does it depend on where they came from, how old they were when they started, or what position they play? This tool follows 7,733 international signees from their first pro season through 2025, using every affiliated level from the Dominican Summer League to the majors.

What the data says · 2007–2016 first seasons

Three things that stand out

2.4×

Starting at 16–17 matters

Players who debuted at 16 or 17 reached the majors at about 9.6%, against 4.0% for those who debuted at 19. Some of that is selection: the top talents sign young in the July 2 class, so age and talent are tangled together. A bonus field would help separate them.

20% vs 17%

Venezuela converts more signees

Venezuelan signees out-reached Dominican signees at every level: 34% vs 31% to full-season ball, 20% vs 17% to AA, and 7.3% vs 6.5% to MLB. The Dominican pool is twice as large, so deeper, higher-volume classes are one likely reason the hit rate per signing is lower.

48%

Shortstops advance early, then level off

Amateur shortstops reached full-season ball at 48%, far above catchers (38%) and pitchers (32%). By MLB the gap nearly closes: 8.0% vs 7.4% for pitchers. Position here is a player's latest listed position, so shortstops who moved off the position count elsewhere. With signing-position data that caveat goes away.

Data & methods

How the data was built

The first job was deciding what the data could be trusted to say. All data comes from MLB's public Stats API.

Pull333,853

player-season rows, every affiliated level, 2000–2025, hitting and pitching, plus bios for 52,746 players

Clean5,512

country values standardized (e.g. "DOM" and "Dominican Republic" were both in use). 9,325 Mexican League rows removed as non-affiliated

Exclude1,967

undrafted players with no birth country, and 94 with no usable position. Counted, not silently dropped

Cohort7,733

international signees whose first pro season was 2007–2016, followed through 2025

  • International signee means never drafted and born outside the U.S., Canada, and Puerto Rico. That captures July 2 amateurs and also older professional signings from Cuba, Japan, and Korea, which is why "21 and older" looks so strong. Use "Amateurs (20 and under)" to set them aside.
  • Starting age is age in the first pro season, a stand-in for signing age. Signing dates and bonuses aren't in the public data.
  • Cohorts start in 2007 because summer-league rosters are incomplete before 2006. Starting a year later keeps anyone who actually began earlier from being mislabeled.
  • Checked against known careers: José Altuve (VSL, 2007, age 17), Salvador Pérez (2007, 17), Rafael Devers (2014, 17), Juan Soto (2016, 17), and Yoán Moncada (2015, 20, straight to Low-A) all land where they should.
  • Duplicate names are kept separate by player ID. There are three different Juan Sotos and several Julio Rodríguezes in the data.

What I would add with club data

  • Signing bonus and bonus pool data, to test value per dollar by country, age, and position, and to separate age from talent.
  • Signing position and scouting grades, so the position view reflects the player the club signed, not the one he became.
  • Pitch-tracking and biomechanical data, since pitchers are the hardest group to read from box-score numbers (see Project 02).
Project 02 · First-season performance

Reading the Summer Leagues

A player's first season in the Dominican or Venezuelan Summer League is the first real stat line a club gets on him. Which of those numbers actually predict who reaches AA and the majors?

Sample: 2,223 hitters (100+ plate appearances) and 2,073 pitchers (100+ batters faced), all international amateurs whose first pro season was in the DSL or VSL from 2007 to 2016. Every stat is measured against that league's average for that season, so a .700 OPS in a low-scoring year counts for more than in a high-scoring one.

Which numbers carry signal

Score is AUC: the chance that a randomly chosen player who made it ranks ahead of one who didn't on that number. 0.50 is a coin flip; 1.00 would be perfect. The combined model is tested on players it wasn't fit to (5-fold cross-validation).

0.72

Strikeout rate is the best single hitter signal

How often a hitter struck out, relative to his league, predicted reaching AA better than OPS, on-base percentage, or power. Hitters in the lowest-strikeout fifth reached the majors at 13%; the highest-strikeout fifth, at 1%.

15×

Young and making contact is the profile

A 16- or 17-year-old who struck out at least 4 points less than his league reached MLB about 20% of the time. The same-age hitter who struck out 4+ points more: 1.3%. Contact at a young age is the strongest early marker in this data.

0.51

Walks tell you almost nothing

Walk rate was close to a coin flip for predicting AA. A likely reason: summer-league pitchers walk batters at high rates, so a DSL walk reflects the pitcher's control as much as the hitter's eye. It's a case where a stat that matters in the majors doesn't translate down.

0.70

Pitchers are harder to read from a box score

The best pitcher model scored 0.70 against 0.83 for hitters. K–BB% was the strongest single number, and the top third reached MLB about three times as often as the bottom third. What's missing is stuff: velocity, spin, and delivery. That's where pitch-tracking and biomechanical data would add the most.

  • These are signals, not causes. Clubs give more playing time to players they already rate highly, which is why playing time itself predicts success. A club's own bonus and scouting data would help separate performance from reputation.
  • Why league-relative: the DSL and VSL change from year to year and differ from each other. Measuring each player against his own league-season keeps those shifts from being mistaken for talent.
  • Minimum sample: 100 plate appearances or batters faced, so one hot or cold week doesn't define a player. Position players and pitchers are separated by their listed position.
Project 03 · The development path

Where the Path Narrows

Project 01 asked who makes it. This one asks where everyone else stops, how long the climb takes, and what a player's age at each level says about his chances.

Sample: 7,300 international amateurs (age 20 or younger in their first pro season) who started between 2007 and 2016, followed through 2025.

33%

The first step is the steepest

Only 1 in 3 international amateurs ever reached full-season ball. Once there, a player’s chance of reaching the majors climbs from 21% at full-season ball to 38% at AA and 53% at AAA. The summer leagues are where most careers end, which is why evaluating that level well matters so much.

2–4 yrs

Most exits come fast

Of players who never reached full-season ball, 92% were done within four pro seasons. Clubs make most of their decisions on international signees within the first few years.

6 yrs

The typical climb takes six seasons

Big leaguers in this group debuted a median of six seasons after their first pro season, usually at 23. That timeline is about the same whether a player started at 16 or 19.

66% vs 23%

Age at AA is a strong tell

Of players who reached AA by age 20, 66% went on to the majors. At 24 or older, 23%. Being young for a level is one of the clearest signals in player development, and this data shows it plainly.

  • Levels follow the affiliated ladder: rookie (DSL, VSL, U.S. complex), short-season A, Low-A, High-A, AA, AAA, MLB. "Full-season" means Low-A or higher.
  • 2020 had no minor league season. Year counts use calendar seasons, so players whose path crossed 2020 show one extra year.
  • Still active: 33 players in the sample are still in the minors without an MLB debut, too few to change the rates.
